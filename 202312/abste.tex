\eabstract{
This study aims to elucidate the long-term citation dynamics and structural characteristics of highly cited papers in the biomedical field. In conventional bibliometrics, short term citation indicators, such as three and five year impact factors, have been primarily used; however, long term observation is essential for capturing the true impact of research papers. In this study, we conducted a multilayered analysis focusing not merely on citation counts, but also on citation lifespan, journal structure, and research topic structure. The data were obtained from the PubMed annual baseline (2023), and the reference information of approximately 30 million papers published between 1946 and 2020 was analyzed. To observe cumulative impact, we constructed a "cumulative generation model" at five year intervals. In each cumulative generation, the top papers accounting for 1\% of the total number of citations were defined as highly cited papers, and changes in their characteristics were examined. In addition, three other groups were established as comparison groups to characterize highly cited papers.
At the paper level, the analysis revealed that, among highly cited papers, some of the most highly cited papers exhibit a long lived pattern in which they do not reach obsolescence (citation decay) even several decades after publication. In contrast, among highly cited papers for which obsolescence was observed, the time from publication to the citation peak was 9.5 years, indicating a tendency toward a longer period than that reported by existing models for the biological sciences.
At the journal level, the analysis showed that journals publishing large numbers of highly cited papers are not necessarily journals with high impact factors. Furthermore, analysis using Kullback–Leibler Divergence revealed that highly cited papers exhibit a stronger tendency to concentrate on, or show a stronger preference for, particular groups of journals compared with the other groups.
Analysis of research topic level, based on entropy measures using MeSH descriptors, revealed that highly cited papers have more concentrated research topics than the other groups, and that the research topics of papers citing them are likewise more concentrated. On the other hand, a divergence emerged between the research topics of highly cited papers and those of the papers citing them from the middle to later cumulative generations, indicating that highly cited papers tend to be cited by papers from different fields more frequently than the other groups.
Finally, regarding the effect of using cumulative citation counts, we found that longer accumulation periods introduce greater variation in intergenerational evaluations, such as rankings.
}
