\eabstract{
This study aims to elucidate the long-term citation dynamics and structural characteristics of highly cited papers in the field of biomedicine. Conventional bibliometrics has primarily relied on short-term citation indicators, such as the 3-year and 5-year Journal Impact Factors; however, long-term observation is essential for understanding the true impact of scientific publications. In this study, we conducted a multilayered analysis focusing not only on citation counts but also on citation longevity, journal structure, and research-topic structure. The data were obtained from the PubMed annual baseline (2023), covering reference information from approximately 30 million papers published between 1946 and 2020. To observe cumulative impact, we constructed a “cumulative generation model” at five-year intervals. Within each cumulative generation, the top papers accounting for 1% of the total citations were defined as “highly cited papers,” and their changes over successive generations were examined. In addition, three other groups were established as comparators to characterize highly cited papers.
At the paper level, we found that highly cited papers exhibited various citation patterns, including “flash-in-the-pan” and “sleeping beauty” types. We also identified long-lived papers that did not reach obsolescence (citation decline) even several decades after publication. Compared with the other groups, highly cited papers included a larger proportion of sleeping beauty-type papers and papers whose citations were still increasing. Among highly cited papers that eventually exhibited obsolescence, the time from publication to the citation peak was 9.5 years, suggesting a longer period than that predicted by existing models for the biological sciences.
At the journal level, the results showed that journals publishing a large number of highly cited papers were not necessarily those with high Journal Impact Factors. Furthermore, an analysis using Kullback–Leibler divergence revealed that highly cited papers showed a stronger concentration, or preference, for particular groups of journals than the other groups.
Regarding research-topic structure, entropy analysis based on MeSH descriptors revealed that highly cited papers were more concentrated in their research topics than the other groups, and that the research topics of papers citing them were also more concentrated. On the other hand, the relationship between the research topics of highly cited papers and those of their citing papers indicated that highly cited papers tended to be cited more frequently by papers from different research fields than did papers in the other groups.
Finally, with regard to the use of cumulative citation counts as a measure of impact, we found that longer observation periods led to greater intergenerational variation in evaluations, including changes in the ranking of papers.
}
